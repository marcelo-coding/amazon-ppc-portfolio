The Pareto Audit: Read All 552 Targets Before You Touch a Bid

Published August 6, 2026 · By Marc Siblag

Before I restructure any account, I read every target it has ever run, because the restructure you design before the audit is just a template, and templates are how money gets lost. This account had 552 PPC targets across its lifetime, with 346 enabled and 129 paused, and I read all of them before I touched a single bid. The audit runs before the restructure and never after it, because the target type economics of an account tell you exactly where the money actually lives and where it only appears to live.

I have spent ten years in Amazon e-commerce, and I manage 15 to 20 accounts at any given time, so I have watched the same pattern repeat in almost every account I open. A small group of targets carries nearly all of the sales, and the long tail mostly holds bids that nobody has questioned in a long time. In this account the concentration was extreme, because phrase match alone produced $84,647.95 of roughly $114K in total PPC sales, which means a single match type carried the entire account.

Those 271 phrase targets ran at 36.91 percent ACOS, and that pairing of huge volume with mediocre efficiency is the whole story of what phrase match is for. Phrase is the discovery workhorse of an account, because it casts a controlled net around a root term and shows you which real customer searches actually convert. But a workhorse is not a destination, so the winners inside those 271 targets need to be mined out into exact match and single keyword campaigns where I control the bid, the budget, and the placement, rather than left to run loose inside a 36.91 percent blend.

The number that surprises people is exact match, because exact ran at 74.87 percent ACOS across 53 targets and only $5,274.21 in sales. Everyone assumes exact match is automatically the efficient tier, but exact is only efficient when the exact terms were chosen from evidence, and these terms were not. Someone had guessed their way into exact match, so the account was paying premium placement for terms that had never proven themselves anywhere. Broad match told a similar story at 55.60 percent ACOS across 21 targets and $6,313.10, because broad without constant harvesting and negation drifts toward whatever Amazon feels like selling you.

The auto campaigns were the quiet professionals of the account, and their 84 targets earned every dollar they spent. Close match ran 41 targets at $11,173.73 in sales and 22.42 percent ACOS, loose match ran 33 targets at $6,721.11 and 15.67 percent, substitutes ran 8 targets at $1,232.64 and 13.47 percent, and complements ran 2 targets at $79.98 and 0.70 percent. Auto match types earn their keep because Amazon does the targeting from its own conversion data, so the account collects sales it never had to research.

The cheapest growth in the entire account was hiding inside product targeting, and almost nobody had bothered to build it. ASIN targets produced $11,470.71 at 19.14 percent ACOS across 109 targets, and category targets produced $1,577.47 at 17.37 percent across 12 more, so the two most efficient manual tactics in the account were also the most underbuilt at 122 total targets. The single Sponsored Display target ran $1,448.59 at 43.06 percent, which reads like an experiment someone launched and then forgot. When the best economics in an account sit in its smallest section, the restructure has already been designed for you.

Every ASIN earns three campaigns

The conclusion I carried out of this audit has become the baseline I now apply everywhere. Every ASIN in an account earns three campaigns before anything else gets built: an auto campaign for close and loose match, a phrase campaign around its core terms, and an ASIN targeting campaign aimed at the competitors it can realistically beat. Those three cover discovery, harvesting, and conquest at the three price points this audit exposed, so every additional campaign in the account has to justify its existence against them.

This is why I keep flagship accounts under 15 percent TACoS, and it has very little to do with bidding harder than the next person. I have managed more than $10M in ad spend, and the lesson that survives all of it is that I refuse to move money until I know where it already lives. Reading all 552 targets is slower than opening a bulk sheet and cutting everything above target ACOS, but the slow read is what showed me that phrase was a mine and not a bleeder, that exact was running on guesses, and that the cheapest growth in the account had barely been built.
