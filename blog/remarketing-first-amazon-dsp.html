A Remarketing First Doctrine for Amazon DSP

Published July 25, 2026 · By Marc Siblag

Amazon DSP gets sold as an awareness machine. The standard pitch climbs down the funnel, with video at the top introducing the brand to strangers and remarketing at the bottom mopping up whatever trickles through. I run it in the opposite direction, and when I open a new DSP flight I start with zero upper funnel spend at all.

Display remarketing carries the volume, and it carries it because the audience has already told you the truth. Shoppers who viewed the product page or abandoned a cart have declared intent with their own behavior, so the job left to do is small and specific, which is to finish a sale that was already in motion. Display is the hammer for that job. The impressions are cheap, the frequency is controllable, and the return is measurable inside a window short enough to act on. Prospecting to cold audiences asks display to do a persuasion job it is bad at, while remarketing asks it to do a reminder job it is excellent at.

The budget follows the doctrine. I run flights on a 60/30/10 split, and the largest share belongs to remarketing display, because the money should sit where the intent already exists. The smaller shares expand outward only from audiences that the remarketing core has already proven can convert.

Video is where discipline matters most, because video is where DSP budgets go to die. My rule is that video has to earn its DSP budget in Sponsored Brands first. The Sponsored Brands video auction gives you fast, cheap, keyword level feedback on whether a creative actually stops a shopper and converts one, and a video that cannot win there is not going to be rescued by DSP inventory at DSP prices. Running unproven video on DSP is just an expensive way to discover that the creative is weak. When a video wins in Sponsored Brands, it has demonstrated demand capture with real purchase data behind it, and only then does it deserve programmatic scale.

The last piece of the doctrine is that products match audiences, not funnel stages. The funnel stage tells you how warm a shopper is, but it says nothing about which product belongs in front of them, and this is where most flight plans quietly go wrong. On one portfolio I ran, we built an audience of medical professionals, and the product that belonged in front of them was the footwear they actually wear on shift rather than the fragrance line from the same catalog, because the audience connection was real for one product and purely aspirational for the other. A nurse standing on a hospital floor for twelve hours has a concrete problem that one of those products solves, and no amount of funnel logic makes the other one relevant.

None of this means the upper funnel is worthless. It means the upper funnel is a privilege you earn. Once remarketing audiences are saturated, once video has proven itself in the sponsored auctions, and once the account is converting everything the lower funnel produces, then climbing toward colder audiences becomes a rational bet instead of a hopeful one. Until that point, every dollar you send upstream is a dollar taken from shoppers who were already reaching for the product, and I have never seen an awareness metric that justifies that trade.
