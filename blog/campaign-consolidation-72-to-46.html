Campaign Consolidation: 72 Campaigns Down to 46 Without Losing a Sale

Published August 6, 2026 · By Marc Siblag

Most of the accounts I inherit arrive with the same disease, which is campaign sprawl. Somebody launched a campaign for every idea, every ASIN, and every seasonal push over the years, and nobody ever closed anything down, so the account I open on day one is a museum of old intentions that are all still spending money. Over ten years in Amazon e-commerce I have run the same consolidation playbook on enough of these accounts to trust it completely, and the version I am going to walk through took one account from 72 active campaigns down to 46 without losing a sale.

The first move is identification, not deletion. I pull the trailing 30 days and find the account's true top performer, the one campaign that actually feeds the business, and I prefix its name with Alpha so nobody who opens the console ever has to guess which campaign matters most. That rename sounds cosmetic, but it changes behavior, because every budget conversation, every bid decision, and every daily check now starts from a shared understanding of where the money comes from.

The second move is the cut, and it is the least emotional decision in the whole playbook. I pause every campaign with zero conversions over two months and beyond, because a campaign that has taken two full months of traffic without producing a single order is no longer an experiment, it is a leak with a name.

Two classes of campaigns never get touched, no matter how messy they make the account look. Single keyword campaigns stay because their history is ranking equity, and ranking equity is the one asset in an ad account that you cannot rebuild simply by writing a bigger check. Inherited campaigns that still convert also stay, because their history is bid-optimization fuel, and every bid decision I make downstream gets smarter when it can draw on a long record of what real shoppers actually did.

The overspenders that survive the zero-conversion test but cannot justify their budgets get quarantined rather than killed. I rename them Bloated, right in the campaign name, so they stay visible to anyone who opens the account and stay reversible the moment one of them earns its way back. Deleting a campaign destroys its history and hides the decision, while a Bloated prefix documents the decision in the one place everyone already looks.

Why fewer campaigns spend more

The counterintuitive result is that the account spent more after the cut, not less. On the account that went from 72 campaigns to 46, I ran the consolidation on June 17, and average daily spend rose from $67 across June 1 to 16 to a sustained $135 to $137 through July 2. That happened because consolidation is not about saving money, it is about unfragmenting budget so the winners can actually spend. Twenty campaigns sharing $100 means no single campaign can win an hour of peak traffic, so the budget dribbles out across auctions that were never going to convert, while one properly funded winner can hold top-of-search through the hours that decide the day.

Fewer campaigns also make every downstream bid decision better, because data density per campaign improves when the same traffic stops scattering. A total of 300 clicks concentrated in one campaign is a signal I can act on, while 15 clicks spread across twenty campaigns is noise that no rule and no human can read. Every bid change I make after a consolidation, whether by hand or through my automation, fires against denser evidence, so the quality of those changes improves as a direct consequence of the structure.

The efficiency shows up fast when the playbook lands on a wasteful account. On another account the same sequence took ACOS from 52 percent to 39 percent within one week, and daily orders held steady the entire time, which is the whole point, because the goal was never to shrink the account, it was to stop paying a fragmentation tax.

I run this playbook across the 15 to 20 accounts I manage concurrently, and it holds up every time because the underlying problem is always the same. Sprawl feels like coverage, but it is really dilution, so the fix is a sequence rather than a purge: I mark the winner, I cut the proven losers, I protect the history that still works, and I label everything else so the account tells its own story to whoever opens it next.
