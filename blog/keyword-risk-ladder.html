The Keyword Risk Ladder: Four Filters I Run on Every Account Every Week

Published August 6, 2026 · By Marc Siblag

Every week I sit down with every account I manage and run the same four filters, in the same order, against the same targets, because risk management on Amazon is a checklist and not a mood. I have spent ten years in Amazon e-commerce and I currently run this discipline across 15 to 20 accounts concurrently, so the filters have to be simple enough to execute at scale and strict enough that nothing bleeding gets to hide. I call the system the keyword risk ladder, and every rung on it is defined by evidence.

Before any filter fires, I pull the same target through six date ranges: lifetime, year to date, 60 days, 30 days, 14 days, and 7 days, and sometimes I add 90 when the account has enough history to make it meaningful. I use multiple windows because a target can look acceptable on its lifetime numbers while it has been bleeding for the last two weeks, and the reverse is just as common, because a target with an ugly lifetime record can be converting right now. Every filter below gets checked against both short and long windows before I act, so a single flattering or ugly view never makes the decision alone.

The four filters, from the top of the ladder down

Filter one catches the worst offenders, which are targets that have spent over $10 with zero sales. The action is a pause, because at that level of spend with nothing to show for it the evidence is overwhelming and the auction has answered the question. This is the only rung on the ladder where I pause anything, and I will explain below why that restraint matters.

Filter two catches targets that have spent over $5 with zero sales and are still enabled. The action is a bid cut of 50 percent, and the cut deepens to 75 percent when the account is running above its ACOS target, because a hot account has no room for patience. When the whole account is over target, every speculative dollar is a dollar the profitable targets have to earn back, so my tolerance for maybes drops with the account.

Filter three works the small end of the ladder, which is spend under $5, zero sales, and at least one click. The action is a bid cut of 15 percent, or 25 on a hot account. The evidence here is thin, since a few clicks without an order proves very little, so the response is a trim rather than a cut, and the target keeps competing while it builds a record.

Filter four is the only one that looks at converting targets, and it flags anything with an ACOS above 50 percent for a bid cut of 15 percent, or 25 on a hot account. A sale does not make a target innocent, because a target that converts at an unaffordable cost is still a leak, and it simply leaks more politely than the zero-sale ones.

There is also a companion rule for the specific case of clicks with no conversions, and it anchors the new bid to the CPC the auction is actually charging. When CPC is at $0.02 I set the bid to $0.02, when the bid sits between $0.02 and $0.10 I set it to CPC minus $0.02, and when the formula result comes out above the current bid I leave the bid alone, because a rule that raises a bid on a target with no conversions has stopped being a risk rule.

The logic that holds the whole ladder together is that severity is proportional to evidence. Pausing only happens at the top of the ladder, where the spend is large and the silence from the auction is unambiguous, and everything below that rung keeps a live bid, because a paused target stops producing information while a reduced target keeps reporting on itself every week. Every run gets logged with screenshots into project management, so there is a permanent audit trail of who cut what and when, and a question about any bid change months later gets answered by a record instead of a memory.

The manual weekly version of this ladder came first, and it ran by hand long enough that every threshold earned its place. Once the rules had survived contact with real accounts across all six windows, they graduated into software, and my automation now runs the same ladder as code. That order matters, because rules you have never run by hand are guesses, while rules you have run for years by hand are policy, and policy is the only thing I am willing to let a machine execute against $10M+ in managed ad spend.
