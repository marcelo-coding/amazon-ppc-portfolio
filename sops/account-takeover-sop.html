Standard Operating Procedure

My Account Takeover SOP

The six step process I run every time I take over an Amazon advertising account, published in full so you can judge the operating system before you ever book a call.

When I take over an Amazon advertising account, I run the same six step process every time. It moves from a fast starting budget through a structured cleanup and into a permanent monthly rhythm, and every step exists because the previous one creates the information the next one needs. The mindset behind all of it is simple. Underspending an approved budget is a missed opportunity even when the ROAS looks good, so once efficiency is proven my job is to grow sales rather than to keep shrinking spend.

Step 1: Set the Budget and the Guardrails

The initial budget is the last 30 days of ad spend plus 10 to 20 percent, and I set it on the very first call so I can start working the account immediately instead of waiting weeks for a perfect number. That extra 10 to 20 percent is deliberate room to maneuver while I digest the account, and I make it clear that this is a starting budget rather than a lifetime budget.

I set the targets, not the brand. Most brands genuinely do not know their own TACoS capacity, so asking them to hand me a target number sets everyone up for failure. What I ask for instead are guardrails, meaning their willingness to spend and their margin constraints, and a useful anchor in that conversation is that brands in a growth phase typically spend 25 to 30 percent of revenue on advertising. From those guardrails and the account historicals I develop the benchmarks and propose the targets myself.

One structural point shapes everything after this. Amazon is a last click platform, so upper funnel advertising will always look worse on ROAS because conversions credit the final click, which is usually a Sponsored Product ad. I never evaluate funnel stages on ROAS alone, because the bottom of the funnel only converts at the rate it does because the top of the funnel feeds it.

Step 2: Score the Account and Build the Forecast

Before I set a single projection I score the account, because the scores determine the growth multiplier and the multiplier determines the forecast. I score four PPC dimensions covering ad spend, cost per click, conversion rate, and organic sales share, working down a condition table from ten until the first condition that matches the account, and I combine them into a composite. Any single score of two or below is a blocking condition regardless of how good the composite looks, and it gets written into the assumptions before anything else happens.

The composite drives how aggressively I project ad driven growth, with a strong composite justifying a multiplier as high as 1.25 on forecasted sales and a merely stable account earning something closer to 1.10. I build a 90 day forecast at the account level and then forecast the top ten products individually, using a competitor proxy discounted by 20 to 30 percent for new products with no history, and the product level total has to land within 10 percent of the account level number or I go find out which products are misestimated.

The most important part of the forecast is the assumptions tab, because the forecast is a conditional projection rather than a promise. Every assumption that must hold is written down with a status, so when a target is missed the miss traces back to a specific broken assumption instead of dissolving into finger pointing.

Step 3: The 21 Day Cleanup

The first 21 days are digestion. I clean up the account and cut wasted spend, and the expected outcome of that work is a lower TACoS with flat or growing sales. At the same time I am learning the account dynamics, meaning the campaign structure, how each campaign type actually functions, where the spend concentrates, and where the money is bleeding, because that knowledge is what makes the targets I propose later realistic instead of theoretical.

The health check I run against account level TACoS is that 15 to 25 percent is the generally healthy zone, with consumables running higher. Stable brands tend to sit around 15 to 18 percent while growth phase brands run around 20 to 25 percent, and I internally anchor accounts at 18 to 20 percent so there are always a few TACoS points left to play with. Budget freed from bleeding campaigns gets reallocated to better performing placements during this window rather than simply banked.

Step 4: The Day 30 Reinvestment Conversation

At roughly day 30 I present the efficiency victory and then immediately convert it into permission to scale, because a lower TACoS is only a victory once. If I keep celebrating declining spend past the first month, the brand learns that I cannot spend their money, and the lower number quietly becomes the new expectation. Budgets work like corporate budgets in that sense, since an unused budget is a budget that gets cut.

So the day 30 conversation does three things. It confirms the true working budget, since the onboarding budget was explicitly provisional. It sets the working TACoS guardrail, which becomes my standing permission to spend. And it frames every point of TACoS I cut as earned experimentation budget, because a TACoS point saved is a TACoS point earned and those points exist to be reinvested into scaling, testing, and upper funnel work rather than left off the table permanently.

Transparency is part of this step too. When an isolated test fails I say exactly what was tried, that it was shut down, and when TACoS will normalize, because a report that says everything is amazing on every single call is what actually creates distrust.

Step 5: Product Level Profitability

Account level TACoS tells me whether there is a problem, while product level analysis tells me where the mobility is, and the inefficiency usually hides in the middle and deep catalog. I pull a product level profit report, flag every product losing money, and make a decision on each one to optimize it, reduce its spend, or stop spending on it entirely. Then I quantify the TACoS points recovered from fixing the losers and reallocate them to best sellers and high margin products, which grows sales while the account level TACoS stays flat.

When cost of goods is missing I estimate it at roughly 40 percent of retail price, since most products are priced a bit over two times their cost, and a product showing losses before cost of goods is even entered is an urgent flag because the real losses are worse. I also hunt explicitly for neglected winners, because a high margin best seller receiving zero ad spend is a missed opportunity that should never survive this review.

The frame I use is margin dollars per unit. If a product clears nine dollars per unit after price, fees, and cost of goods, then nine dollars is the ceiling on what I can pay to acquire a customer, and the goal is to operate as close to the accepted cost per customer as possible rather than as far below it as possible.

Step 6: Monthly Reviews and Events

The day 30 reassessment repeats forever as a monthly review. By the third business day of each month the prior month's actuals go into the tracking sheet, and a result within plus or minus 10 percent of forecast means the forecast holds while anything outside that band gets investigated. I re score the PPC metrics on current data, and any score that moved by two or more points forces a re evaluation of the growth multiplier for the remaining forecast period. I only ever adjust forward months, because the original forecast has to stay visible for variance tracking to be honest.

Major shopping events get their own playbook layered on top of this rhythm. Before an event like Prime Day I take a full bulksheet backup as the rollback artifact before touching anything, identify top performers and deal products, re enable proven but paused campaigns at conservative bids and budgets, remove dayparting, and set account level budget caps, with all increases staged in advance and a final readiness check locked in before the event opens. During the event the account runs on a daily cadence of monitoring, feeding winners, and cutting bleeders, with a mid event strategic review setting up the back half. After the event the elevated posture reverses in stages using the event tracker as the source of truth, so the account lands back at its normal settings deliberately instead of by memory.

Once the takeover is complete, the account moves onto the permanent cadence documented in my weekly optimization SOP, and the results this process produces are on the case studies page.
